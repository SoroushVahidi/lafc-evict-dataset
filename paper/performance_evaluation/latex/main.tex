\begin{abstract}
\begin{sloppypar}
Evaluating cache-eviction decisions requires knowing what would have
happened under each candidate choice, information that is not directly
observable from a single trace replay. This paper studies \lafc, a
reusable way to construct that counterfactual evidence at production
scale -- a per-decision, per-candidate finite-horizon counterfactual miss
cost, computed for real cache traces -- and evaluates it against five
questions. Is the target actually discriminative between candidates, and
when does it degenerate? Across five trace families, four capacities, and
three horizons, we find the target is not uniformly discriminative:
two-thirds of decisions are fully tied in aggregate and no audited decision
has a strict unique winner, but discriminativeness varies strongly and
predictably by workload, capacity, and horizon, and one workload is a
fully degenerate, mechanistically explained negative control. Does the
offline candidate ranking correspond to actual closed-loop cache
performance? A production-scale closed-loop evaluation (LRU, MRU, random,
SIEVE; 5 families $\times$ 2 capacities $\times$ 20 seeds) shows offline
and closed-loop policy orderings agree in most workload/capacity regimes
where the offline signal is not near zero, with two specific, characterized
exceptions. How does this depend on workload, capacity, and horizon?
Capacity-scale reuse in the underlying trace predicts both offline
discriminativeness and closed-loop policy separation ($r=0.83$--$0.97$,
$n=10$, exploratory), and a horizon of 16 requests aligns offline and
closed-loop evidence more strongly than shorter horizons. How sensitive
are the labels to the continuation-policy assumption used to construct
them? A pre-registered, stratified 5,500-decision sampled study at
capacities 32 and 128 finds both MRU and mean-random continuation classify
robust, and a separate full-population MRU census over capacities 32, 64,
128, and 256 also satisfies the same robustness criterion. Random
continuation is measurably more perturbing than MRU in the sample, and no
population-scale random census is claimed; we report this with its exact
scope rather than as a blanket robustness claim.
What does this imply in practice? We argue, and the evidence here
supports, that this offline supervision complements rather than replaces
closed-loop evaluation, and give concrete guidance for when the offline
signal can be trusted. \lafc\ also releases the underlying candidate-level,
decision-level, and pairwise supervision (277,995,072 candidate rows,
2,363,286 decisions, five trace families) for reuse by other researchers.
Every quantitative claim in this paper traces to an independently
validated, gate-checked artifact, including the completed and independently
rechecked full-population MRU continuation census.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{abstract}
