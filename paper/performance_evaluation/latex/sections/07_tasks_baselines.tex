\section{Benchmark Tasks and Baselines}
\label{sec:tasks-baselines}

\lafc\ supports four benchmark tasks from one release.

\paragraph{Candidate-level value prediction.}
Predict \texttt{y\_loss} or \texttt{y\_value} from a single candidate row -- the pointwise view of finite-horizon counterfactual eviction quality (Table~\ref{tab:task-definitions}).

\paragraph{Best-candidate selection.}
Given all candidate rows for one decision, identify a member of the minimum-loss set. This task can be framed as ranking or decision-level classification and connects most directly to the actual cache-eviction choice because it evaluates whether a method selects a benchmark-optimal victim within that decision. We call a decision \textbf{discriminative} when \texttt{optimal\_candidate\_count < candidate\_count}, i.e.\ at least one candidate is strictly worse than the best candidate in that decision; on any other (all-tied) decision every candidate is optimal by construction, so no selector's choice can affect realized regret there. Because of this, all-decision optimal-selection rate and mean regret are reported below only as \emph{secondary, ecological} statistics describing the full target distribution, and selector performance restricted to discriminative decisions is the \textbf{primary} best-candidate-selection metric.

\paragraph{Pairwise preference prediction.}
Predict whether candidate A is better than candidate B within the same decision, where ``better'' means lower \texttt{y\_loss} or equivalently higher \texttt{y\_value}. This task uses the pairwise view or shipped pairwise sample as a simpler interface for within-decision preference modeling. Because equal losses induce ties, reports must state whether evaluation includes ties, excludes them, or treats them as a separate class. In the shipped sample, deterministic hash randomization of A/B orientation keeps the non-tie A-better/B-better split balanced (about 49.8\%/50.2\%) rather than encoding candidate-identifier order.

\paragraph{Candidate-label distribution characterization.}
Summarize the empirical label surface -- distributions of \texttt{y\_loss}, \texttt{y\_value}, regret, and tie frequency across families, capacities, horizons, and splits -- without training a predictive model; this descriptive task underpins interpretation of the other three (Table~\ref{tab:task-definitions}).

The empirical scope of this paper remains intentionally narrow. Rather than presenting a large model bake-off, we report lightweight baselines that can be executed directly from the committed release views and that establish reproducible reference points for later work.

\begin{sloppypar}
The current results surface covers three such references. A linear regression baseline over candidate rows predicts \texttt{y\_loss} from released features. It is intentionally weak: test MAE is 3.8739 with RMSE 4.7152 and $R^2=0.0026$, and the validation-split $R^2$ is slightly negative ($-0.0506$). Using that same linear score for within-decision selection nevertheless provides a reproducible decision-level floor. On the pairwise task, the earlier random and majority non-tie results remain useful sanity checks, but by themselves they do not test whether the released candidate features contain decision-relevant signal.
\end{sloppypar}

To test whether released candidate features carry pairwise signal without
changing the benchmark schema, we join those features back to the capped
shipped pairwise sample for evaluation only. This remains a sample-backed
evaluation, not a full quadratic pairwise materialization. The canonical
regenerated sample is itself tie-heavy: 878,262 of 1,000,000 rows (87.8\%)
are exact ties, leaving 121,738 non-ties. All classifier metrics below are
therefore non-tie-only.

\textbf{Fitting and evaluation protocol.} Logistic score calibrations and
the feature-difference model are fit only on non-tie \texttt{train} rows,
with fixed L2 regularization and no validation tuning. The temporally
held-out \texttt{test} split is the generalization result; train/validation
columns are diagnostic. On the 2,211-row test non-tie subset, the logistic
feature-difference model reaches 0.7078 accuracy (Wilson 95\% CI
$[0.688, 0.726]$) and 0.5494 log loss,
ahead of random/majority checks and the 1-D score baselines
(Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-feature-baselines}). This corrected test
non-tie accuracy represents the authoritative baseline for pairwise
generalization on the held-out split. The train/validation
gap is large, and the test non-tie subset is small and unusually tie-heavy,
so the result supports only a capped-sample, non-tie pairwise baseline,
not a population or online-policy claim.

\begin{sloppypar}
This test non-tie subset is also highly workload-concentrated: twemcache
and alibaba-block together supply 94.98\% of it (58.16\% and 36.82\%
respectively, of 2,211 rows), while metacdn contributes zero test
non-ties because metacdn has zero decisions of any kind in the test
split at the decision-view level (Section~\ref{sec:characterization}) --
a full-family train/val/test partitioning property, not an artifact of
pairwise sampling. wiki2018 likewise contributes no test non-ties,
consistent with its complete tie-degeneracy. The 0.7078 result therefore
demonstrates decision-relevant signal on the available held-out non-tie
population, not generalization across all five workload families. We
found no evidence of feature leakage: none of the 26 candidate-level
feature columns used here reference \texttt{y\_loss}, \texttt{y\_value},
regret, or any split-identity quantity. Separately, 18 of those 26 --
\texttt{candidate\_predictor\_score} and seventeen related predictor-,
bucket-, and confidence-derived fields (discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) -- are hard-coded placeholder constants
across the entire release, so after standardization their feature
difference is exactly zero and they contribute no decision signal to
the model. A further feature, \texttt{candidate\_is\_predictor\_victim},
is bit-identical to \texttt{candidate\_is\_lru\_victim} and is therefore
exactly redundant. In total, 19 of the 26 supplied features provide no
additional independent variation, leaving seven that are nonconstant
and nonredundant; this establishes only nonconstancy, not that those
seven carry independent predictive signal.
The large train (0.9598) / validation (0.9896) versus test (0.7078)
accuracy gap is most consistent with workload/split distribution shift,
given the test set's family concentration described above, rather than a
demonstrated leakage or sampling-bug mechanism; we have not established
this mechanism conclusively and do not claim more than that here.
\end{sloppypar}

The best-candidate evaluator groups candidate rows by the canonical
9-column decision key and reports 2,363,286 decisions, an exact match
with the decision view (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-design}). A cheap
decision-level floor computed directly from the decision view shows why
this match matters for interpretation: a uniform-random selector's
expected optimal-selection rate is 0.9912 with expected regret 0.0088,
both more favorable than the linear-score selector's observed 0.8753
optimal-selection rate and 0.1251 mean regret, purely because of the tie
density documented in Section~\ref{sec:characterization} -- not because
the linear score underperforms a stronger baseline. These all-decision
figures are secondary, ecological statistics; headline selection-rate
and regret numbers should therefore never be read in isolation from
either the random floor or the discriminative-only figures below.

764,282 of 2,363,286 decisions (32.34\%) are discriminative, per the
definition above. Restricted to
this stratum -- the only decisions on which a selector's choice can
affect realized regret -- the uniform-random selector's expected
optimal-selection rate falls to 0.9729 (expected regret 0.0273), while
the linear-score selector's optimal-selection rate falls to 0.6145 (mean
regret 0.3867). These discriminative-only figures follow exactly from the
aggregate all-decision totals, because every all-tied decision necessarily
contributes optimal-selection~$=1$ and regret~$=0$ for any selector.
On the decisions where the finite-horizon target actually distinguishes
candidates, the linear score is therefore substantially worse than
uniform random -- not merely comparable to it -- and we report this
directly rather than only the more favorable all-decision figures. We do
not rescue this baseline with an alternative normalization or by
excluding difficult cells; Table~\ref{tab:label-selection-summary}
reports both views side by side.
