\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\begin{sloppypar}
When a cache request misses and the cache is full, a system must pick one
resident object to evict before admitting the requested object. Evaluating
whether a given eviction decision was a good one requires knowing what
would have happened to each candidate victim if it, rather than another
object, had been evicted. That information is not directly observable
from a single trace replay, because only one candidate is actually evicted
at each decision. This paper studies a reusable way to construct that
counterfactual evidence at scale. It evaluates when the evidence is
informative, when it degenerates, how it relates to actual closed-loop
cache performance, and how sensitive it is to one of its own modeling
assumptions.
\end{sloppypar}

\lafc\ materializes, for real cache traces, a per-decision, per-candidate
finite-horizon counterfactual miss cost. For each object that could have
been evicted at a full-cache miss, the cost counts how many additional
misses would occur over the next $H$ requests if that object,
specifically, had been the one evicted. This candidate-level quantity is
stored as \texttt{y\_loss} (a
convenience alias \texttt{y\_value} $= -\texttt{y\_loss}$ is also provided;
see Section~\ref{sec:tasks-baselines}). Section~\ref{sec:background} works
through one full decision by hand -- resident objects, a forced eviction, a
future request window, and the resulting candidate costs, including a case
where two candidates tie for optimal -- before any aggregate statistic is
introduced. This grounds every quantity used later in the paper in a
concrete example first.

\begin{sloppypar}
The contribution of this paper is not a new eviction policy. It is an
evaluation of a reusable offline supervision surface against closed-loop
cache-policy behavior, organized around five questions.
\textbf{(RQ1)} Is the target discriminative between candidates, and when
does it degenerate to an uninformative tie? Not uniformly: aggregate tie
rates are substantial and no audited decision has a strict unique winner,
but discriminativeness varies strongly and predictably by workload,
capacity, and horizon, and one workload is a genuine, mechanistically
explained negative control.
\textbf{(RQ2)} Does the offline ranking correspond to actual closed-loop
performance? In most workload/capacity regimes where the offline signal
is not near zero, yes, with two specific, characterized exceptions.
\textbf{(RQ3)} What drives that correspondence, and does it depend on
horizon? Capacity-scale locality in the underlying trace correlates with
both offline discriminativeness and closed-loop policy separation. Among
the three horizons this paper evaluates ($H\in\{4,8,16\}$), $H=16$ aligns
offline and closed-loop evidence most strongly, though modestly; this
paper also includes a separate long-horizon sensitivity study showing that
the target remains substantially tied through $H=128$.
\textbf{(RQ4)} How sensitive are the labels to the continuation policy
assumed after a forced eviction? Robust to the tested alternatives: a
sampled study at two capacities finds the labels robust to MRU and
mean-random continuation by a pre-registered criterion. A full-population
census extends the MRU result across all four evaluated capacities --
arbitrary continuation policies remain outside this claim.
\textbf{(RQ5)} What does this mean in practice for a researcher choosing
between offline and closed-loop evaluation? The offline supervision this
dataset provides complements rather than replaces closed-loop evaluation;
we give concrete, evidenced guidance for when the offline signal can be
trusted. Taken together, these answers quantify when finite-horizon
counterfactual supervision is discriminative, continuation-stable, and
aligned -- or measurably misaligned -- with closed-loop policy behavior
across workloads and capacities.
\end{sloppypar}

Every quantitative claim in this paper traces to a specific, independently
validated evidence artifact. Sections~\ref{sec:tasks-baselines}
through~\ref{sec:characterization} and the limitations discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} state the exact scope, sample sizes, and
caveats attached to each result rather than presenting them as unqualified
findings. The underlying release also exposes candidate-level, decision-level,
and pairwise derived views of the same supervision at production scale
(Table~\ref{tab:dataset-scale}), which supports the evaluation methodology
in this paper and is also intended for reuse by other researchers studying
learned cache eviction. Release construction, schema, and validation are
described in Sections~\ref{sec:benchmark-design}--\ref{sec:release-validation},
but are not the paper's central argument.

Section~\ref{sec:related-work} positions this contribution precisely
against learned-caching, benchmark, and offline-vs-deployed-evaluation
literature. In brief, existing public cache-replacement resources do not
provide a reusable candidate-level counterfactual supervision surface at
this scale together with a direct evaluation of its relationship to
closed-loop cache-policy behavior.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
gives the worked example and motivates decision-aligned counterfactual
supervision. Sections~\ref{sec:benchmark-design}--\ref{sec:release-validation}
describe construction, label definition, schema, and release validation.
Section~\ref{sec:tasks-baselines} defines the benchmark tasks. Section~\ref{sec:characterization}
reports target discriminativeness (RQ1) and, together with material drawn
from the closed-loop production evaluation, addresses RQ2--RQ4 (offline/
closed-loop correspondence, workload/capacity/horizon dependence, and
continuation-policy robustness); practical implications (RQ5) are
synthesized alongside it. Section~\ref{sec:learned-closed-loop} then reports
a separate, frozen learned-policy closed-loop check under its own held-out
protocol. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states limitations,
ethics, and reuse constraints, followed by related work and conclusion.
