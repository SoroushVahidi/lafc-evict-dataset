\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\begin{sloppypar}
When a cache request misses and the cache is full, a system must pick one
resident object to evict before admitting the requested object. Evaluating
whether a given eviction decision was a good one requires knowing what
would have happened to each candidate victim if it, rather than another
object, had been evicted. That information is not directly observable
from a single trace replay, because only one candidate is actually evicted
at each decision. This paper studies a reusable way to construct that
counterfactual evidence at scale. It evaluates when the evidence is
informative, when it degenerates, how it relates to actual closed-loop
cache performance, and how sensitive it is to one of its own modeling
assumptions.
\end{sloppypar}

\lafc\ materializes, for real cache traces, a per-decision, per-candidate
finite-horizon counterfactual miss cost. For each object that could have
been evicted at a full-cache miss, the cost counts how many additional
misses would occur over the next $H$ requests if that object,
specifically, had been the one evicted. This candidate-level quantity is
stored as \texttt{y\_loss} (a
convenience alias \texttt{y\_value} $= -\texttt{y\_loss}$ is also provided;
see Section~\ref{sec:tasks-baselines}). Section~\ref{sec:background} works
through one full decision by hand -- resident objects, a forced eviction, a
future request window, and the resulting candidate costs, including a case
where two candidates tie for optimal -- before any aggregate statistic is
introduced. This grounds every quantity used later in the paper in a
concrete example first.

\begin{sloppypar}
The contribution of this paper is not a new eviction policy. It is an
evaluation of a reusable offline supervision surface against closed-loop
cache-policy behavior, organized around five research questions:
\textbf{(RQ1) Is the target discriminative between candidates?} We find massive
tie-degeneracy overall, but discriminativeness varies predictably by workload,
capacity, and horizon.
\textbf{(RQ2 \& RQ3) Does the offline ranking correspond to actual closed-loop
performance, and what drives it?} Offline and closed-loop orderings agree in
most regimes where the offline signal is not near zero, driven heavily by
capacity-scale locality in the underlying trace.
\textbf{(RQ4) How sensitive are the labels to the assumed continuation policy?}
A pre-registered study finds the canonical labels robust to MRU and random
continuation, though random continuation is measurably more perturbing.
\textbf{(RQ5) What does this mean in practice?} Offline supervision complements
rather than replaces closed-loop evaluation; we provide concrete, evidenced
guidance for when the offline signal can be trusted. Taken together, these
answers quantify when finite-horizon counterfactual supervision is aligned
-- or measurably misaligned -- with closed-loop policy behavior.
\end{sloppypar}

Every quantitative claim in this paper traces to a specific, independently
validated evidence artifact. Sections~\ref{sec:tasks-baselines}
through~\ref{sec:learned-closed-loop} and the limitations discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} state the exact scope, sample sizes, and
caveats attached to each result. The underlying release also exposes candidate-level, decision-level,
and pairwise derived views of the same supervision at production scale
(Table~\ref{tab:dataset-scale}), intended for reuse by other researchers studying
learned cache eviction.

Section~\ref{sec:related-work} positions this contribution precisely
against learned-caching, benchmark, and offline-vs-deployed-evaluation
literature. In brief, existing public cache-replacement resources do not
provide a reusable candidate-level counterfactual supervision surface at
this scale together with a direct evaluation of its relationship to
closed-loop cache-policy behavior.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
gives the worked example. Sections~\ref{sec:benchmark-design}--\ref{sec:release-validation}
describe construction, schema, and release validation. Section~\ref{sec:tasks-baselines}
defines the benchmark tasks. Section~\ref{sec:characterization}
reports target discriminativeness and, together with the closed-loop production evaluation,
addresses offline/closed-loop correspondence and continuation-policy robustness.
Section~\ref{sec:learned-closed-loop} reports a separate frozen learned-policy closed-loop
check. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states limitations, followed by related work and conclusion.
