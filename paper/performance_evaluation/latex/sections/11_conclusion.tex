\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\lafc\ is a benchmark artifact for learned cache eviction, not a new
eviction policy: a released, validated candidate-level counterfactual
label, together with decision and pairwise views built on top of it. The
paper's contribution is evaluating that label rather than assuming it is
useful. The label turns out to be conditionally, not uniformly,
informative, and its informativeness is explained, not just measured: it
tracks a single trace-level property, capacity-scale reuse, closely enough
to predict both where the offline signal is strong and where closed-loop
policies actually separate. Direct closed-loop replay -- now across seven
policies, not four -- shows this offline$\leftrightarrow$closed-loop
correspondence holds in most, not all, regimes, with every exception
identified and investigated rather than smoothed over; relabeling the same
decisions under MRU and random continuation, at both a sampled and a
full-population scale, changes almost nothing about which candidate is
optimal.

\begin{sloppypar}
These results support conservative use. The offline label is a finite-horizon
counterfactual under one continuation policy, not a globally optimal
online target, and every quantitative claim above carries the scope stated
in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}: an $n{=}8$--10 linkage sample, continuation
robustness tested only for MRU and random at the evaluated capacities, and
a seven-policy closed-loop comparison that is representative, not
exhaustive. Within that scope, \lafc\ gives a researcher a reproducible way
to ask whether a candidate-level eviction signal is informative before
building a policy around it, and concrete guidance (Section~\ref{sec:practical-implications})
for when the answer can be trusted. Extending that guidance to more
continuation policies, a trained (not merely tested) learned selector under
closed-loop replay, and broader public hosting are the natural next steps.
\end{sloppypar}
