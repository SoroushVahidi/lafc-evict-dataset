\subsection{Classical and Heuristic Cache Replacement}

Classical cache-replacement work defines the policy landscape that any learned benchmark must eventually interface with. Belady's optimal look-ahead policy remains the canonical offline reference point~\cite{belady1966study}. Practical online policies then emphasize different signals: LIRS focuses on inter-reference recency~\cite{jiang2002lirs}, ARC adaptively balances recency and frequency~\cite{megiddo2003arc}, CLOCK-Pro revisits CLOCK-style replacement with stronger reuse sensitivity~\cite{jiang2005clockpro}, and GreedyDual-Size accounts for cost and size~\cite{cao1997greedydual}. TinyLFU and W-TinyLFU further show that admission and segmented cache management matter in modern software caches~\cite{einziger2017tinylfu,einziger2018wtinylfu}. These works provide policies and design intuitions, but they do not release a public benchmark artifact with one labeled row per eviction candidate or reusable pairwise preference data.

\subsection{Recent Simple and Scalable Object-Cache Policies}

Recent object-cache work also shows that policy progress does not always come from heavier machinery. S3-FIFO argues that simple FIFO-based queueing can beat more elaborate baselines at scale~\cite{yang2023s3fifo}, while SIEVE emphasizes turn-key deployability for web caches~\cite{zhang2024sieve}. These papers are especially relevant because they target production-style object and web caching rather than only theoretical paging models. Their contribution, however, is still a policy contribution: each proposes an eviction rule and evaluates it as an endpoint. \lafc\ instead publishes a reusable supervision artifact on which such policies, or learned surrogates inspired by them, can later be studied without presenting \lafc\ itself as another eviction policy.

\subsection{Learned Cache Replacement and Imitation Learning}

Recent learned-caching papers likewise contribute learned policies or learning frameworks rather than benchmark releases. RL-Cache studies learned cache admission for content delivery~\cite{kirilin2020rlcache}. Parrot uses imitation learning to approximate Belady-style replacement decisions from past accesses~\cite{liu2020parrot}. MAT focuses on reducing the overhead of ML-guided eviction by using a heuristic filter around a learned module~\cite{yang2023mat}. ALPS emphasizes lightweight learned replacement and the practical cost of learning overheads~\cite{shahout2024alps}, while Feedforward Neural Networks for Caching is useful precisely because it questions whether heavier learned models are always justified~\cite{fedchenko2018fnncache}. HR-Cache extends the learned-caching line into edge delivery settings~\cite{torabi2024hrcache}. The distinction for this paper is therefore the unit of contribution: prior work mainly proposes and evaluates policies or policy-learning frameworks, whereas \lafc\ contributes a release-oriented benchmark with candidate-victim supervision that later policy-learning work can reuse.

\subsection{Learning-Augmented Caching and Predictive Advice}

Another relevant line of work treats caching as an online problem augmented by predictions rather than as a supervised dataset. Competitive Caching with Machine Learned Advice and later learning-augmented caching results formalize how predictive advice can improve classical online algorithms while preserving robustness guarantees~\cite{lykouris2021mladvice,wei2020learningaugcache}. This perspective is close in spirit because it also separates policy decisions from predictive signals, but the objects of study differ. Learning-augmented caching papers typically reason about next-arrival-style advice and competitive guarantees for online eviction algorithms. By contrast, \lafc\ releases finite-horizon, continuation-policy-dependent counterfactual labels for every candidate within a decision. The artifact supports value regression, best-candidate selection, pairwise preference learning, and label characterization from one release, rather than prescribing one online policy with one advice interface.

\subsection{Benchmark and Dataset Positioning}

Public traces and benchmark infrastructure are equally important prior art. The UMass Trace Repository and large-scale workload studies of key-value stores and Twemcache clusters demonstrate the value of realistic, heterogeneous workload data for systems evaluation~\cite{umasstracerepo,atikoglu2012kvworkload,yang2020twemcache}. More broadly, reusable benchmark artifacts such as YCSB, OLTP-Bench, and the Join Order Benchmark made comparison easier by publishing standard workload or evaluation surfaces rather than only one-off experimental setups~\cite{cooper2010ycsb,difallah2013oltpbench,leis2018job}. Public learning-to-rank collections such as LETOR and the Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge likewise show how reusable labeled artifacts can organize a research community~\cite{liu2009ltr,qin2010letor,chapelle2011yahoo}.

Those contributions, however, stop at a different layer than \lafc. Raw traces expose requests and workload structure; benchmark harnesses expose workloads or evaluation tasks; learning-to-rank datasets expose reusable supervision but not cache-decision semantics. The label design in \lafc\ also connects to counterfactual learning and ranking because each cache decision induces a candidate set, an optimal subset, regret summaries, and a natural pairwise structure~\cite{swaminathan2015crm,li2011offlinecb,joachims2017unbiasedltr,saito2020pairwise}. Even so, \lafc\ is not simply a ranking dataset transplanted into systems. Its labels are tied to finite-horizon eviction outcomes under a specified continuation policy, and they are not globally optimal online decisions. To our knowledge, existing public cache-replacement artifacts do not provide a reusable candidate-level counterfactual supervision table at this scale. That is the central differentiation the paper needs to maintain throughout: \lafc\ is not a new policy and not merely a raw trace repository, but a reusable benchmark artifact for learned cache-eviction research.
