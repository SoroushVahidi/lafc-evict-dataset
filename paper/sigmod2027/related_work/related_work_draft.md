# Related Work Draft

## Classical cache replacement

The classical cache-replacement literature provides the algorithmic baseline for any learned cache-eviction benchmark. Belady’s optimal look-ahead analysis established the canonical upper-bound reference for replacement quality, even though it is not implementable online. Subsequent practical policies focused on approximating different notions of locality or utility under realistic constraints. LIRS refined recency-based replacement by using inter-reference recency rather than plain stack position. ARC introduced an adaptive policy that balances recency and frequency without fixed tuning. CLOCK-Pro retained the practical flavor of CLOCK while incorporating ideas from reuse-sensitive policies. GreedyDual-Size brought cost and object size into replacement, which is especially relevant when eviction quality cannot be reduced to hit count alone. TinyLFU and later W-TinyLFU emphasized cache admission and practical software-cache management, showing that frequency-aware filtering and segmented designs can substantially improve performance in modern software caches.

These works are essential context, but they address a different problem from the one in this paper. They propose or analyze policies. They do not release a public benchmark artifact with one labeled row per candidate eviction at each decision, nor do they provide reusable decision-level and pairwise supervised-learning tasks derived from the same release. In this sense, \lafc\ should be read as complementary to the classical literature: it creates a benchmark surface on which those policies, or models inspired by them, can be studied more systematically.

## Learned caching and ML-based eviction

More recent work applies learning directly to cache management. RL-Cache treats cache admission for content delivery as a reinforcement-learning problem. ALPS argues that learned cache management must account for the overhead of the learning machinery itself and proposes a lightweight learned replacement strategy derived from sketch-based features. Feedforward Neural Networks for Caching is especially useful as a cautionary reference because it shows that neural predictors can improve over standard heuristics while also questioning whether model complexity is always justified. HR-Cache extends the learned-caching line into edge content delivery and frames its method around learning from hazard-rate-based eviction structure.

Taken together, these papers show that cache management is now a legitimate machine-learning target rather than an exclusively heuristic one. However, the public output of those efforts is still typically a policy paper: the unit of contribution is the algorithm and its evaluation, not a reusable labeled dataset. Even when public traces or code are available, the artifact generally does not expose per-candidate counterfactual loss labels, finite-horizon value targets, or multiple benchmark task views. \lafc\ differs by centering the release artifact itself. The goal is not to claim a better policy than RL-Cache, ALPS, or HR-Cache, but to make supervised and ranking-style evaluation of cache-eviction decisions more comparable across future methods.

## Cache traces and systems benchmarks

Another important body of prior work concerns traces, workloads, and benchmark infrastructure. Public trace resources such as the UMass Trace Repository have long supported systems evaluation by making storage and network traces available to the community. In caching specifically, workload-analysis papers on large-scale key-value stores and Twitter’s Twemcache clusters have been valuable because they expose the diversity, skew, and operational scale of production cache workloads. These works matter for \lafc\ because they show that realistic cache evaluation depends strongly on workload heterogeneity rather than on one synthetic access pattern.

Still, raw or semi-processed traces alone do not solve the benchmark problem addressed here. A trace repository is not yet a decision-aligned supervised benchmark. It may contain requests, sizes, or timing, but it does not necessarily provide the structured candidate sets, counterfactual labels, or task splits needed for candidate scoring, best-candidate prediction, and pairwise preference learning. For that reason, \lafc\ is also related to broader benchmark packages in data management and systems, such as YCSB, OLTP-Bench, and the Join Order Benchmark. Those artifacts succeed because they create reusable evaluation surfaces rather than isolated experiments. The benchmark ambition of \lafc\ is closest to that tradition, but specialized to learned cache eviction and to counterfactual supervision rather than transaction processing or query optimization.

## Counterfactual supervision and learning-to-rank

The supervision design in \lafc\ is also connected to the literatures on counterfactual learning, offline evaluation, and learning to rank. Counterfactual Risk Minimization and contextual-bandit offline evaluation provide methodological precedent for learning from logged or partial feedback without requiring online deployment of each candidate policy. Unbiased learning-to-rank extends that line of work to ranking under biased interaction feedback. On the benchmark side, the learning-to-rank literature has also developed public datasets and evaluation cultures that are directly relevant here. Liu’s survey remains a standard reference for pointwise, pairwise, and listwise ranking views, while LETOR and the Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge exemplify how a public dataset can structure a research subcommunity.

These references are useful because \lafc\ is not purely a regression dataset. Each cache decision induces a candidate set, an optimal subset, regret values, and a natural pairwise comparison structure. That makes ranking-oriented evaluation central rather than incidental. At the same time, the benchmark is not simply a transfer of learning-to-rank machinery into systems. The rows are not web documents or recommendation items; they are candidate victims within cache-eviction decisions, and the labels are finite-horizon counterfactual costs under a continuation policy. To our knowledge, we are not aware of prior public caching datasets that provide per-candidate finite-horizon counterfactual eviction labels at this scale.

## Benchmark and dataset contributions

Finally, \lafc\ should be situated among benchmark and dataset contributions rather than only among policy papers. The closest analogies are not other cache policies, but public artifacts that changed how results are compared: YCSB for cloud serving systems, OLTP-Bench for database workloads, JOB for query optimization, and LETOR or Yahoo! LTR for ranking. What these efforts share is that they make evaluation itself more reusable. They define interfaces, tasks, splits, metrics, and sometimes data distributions that outlive any single model or heuristic.

\lafc\ follows that benchmark-paper model, but for learned cache eviction. It is not a new eviction policy, and it is not merely a raw trace repository. It is a decision-aligned, counterfactual supervision benchmark that exposes candidate-level labels together with decision-level and pairwise task views, plus release metadata, checksums, validation code, and public-facing documentation. That release-oriented structure is the central point of departure from prior cache-replacement and learned-caching work.
